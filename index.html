SHEET 00 / DELIVERY SYSTEM

How I work — the CPDF framework

A delivery system I designed and now run in production: every strategic initiative gets a living document that starts before the first commit and stays alive after deployment. Not retroactive documentation — the source of truth, written first and updated throughout.

CPDF — Continuous Product Discovery Framework · 14 initiatives run through it to date
Nothing ships without a business case.
No strategic initiative starts writing code before its document exists. Half the initiatives needed several rounds of reasoning before converging on an approach worth building.
Every initiative closes with an honest reflection.
What we'd change, what risks we missed, whether the metrics actually moved. It's what turns a shipped feature into knowledge the next one can use.
Documented state beats remembered state.
A full audit of 190 commits found the old docs described 7 of 44 migrations and listed shipped work as "upcoming." Now the inventory is rebuilt from the code, not from memory.
SHEET 01–04 / SELECTED WORK

Selected work

Four cases, ordered the way a roadmap gets defended in front of a business: create the value, protect it, scale it safely, then make it compound. Two are from the SaaS platform I own; two are from client delivery.

Valuethe recurring revenue loop
An alert engine that reads every client's ERP like a consultant would
AI Orchestration / Platform
Challenge
Once an Odoo implementation shipped, the relationship went quiet — the client only reappeared when something broke. A subscription portal that just displayed KPIs would be a mirror: nice in month one, ignored by month three. It needed to tell clients something they didn't already know.
Approach
Five layers, each shipped to production before starting the next: deterministic rules → a curated opportunity catalog with real checks against the client's ERP → adaptive per-client baselines (each client measured against its own 90-day normal, not a global threshold) → AI-generated contextual alerts, every number verified against real data by deterministic code before a human sees it → a transparent priority queue. Then the conversion layer: alerts become a client-facing proposal, with an explicit human publish gate and a deliberately non-AI draft generator.
Verified AI cost: $0.05 per client generation
Validated on 4 real clients, not test data
Human review gate: 100% of client-facing output
Outcome
The "AI proposes, code verifies, human decides" pattern was born here and became the standard architecture for everything on the platform that generates client-facing text — including the strategic report generator, where that same verification layer has already caught and blocked an AI-generated report before it reached a client. Clients now get a monthly reason to log in that isn't a support ticket.
What I'd do differently
Built in the wrong order — three layers of detection before anyone asked who reviews the output. Alerts piled up unread until the priority queue shipped. A signal with no owner and no deadline isn't a feature, it's a backlog.

Protectintegrity of the business number
A price that was never decided, hardcoded in twelve places
Data Governance
Challenge
A routine question — "is this price right?" — surfaced that the platform's tiering was written literally into 12 points across 6 files, including the commercial PDF sent to clients. The top-tier price had, in fact, never been decided by anyone; the code had simply been asserting it to customers for months.
Approach
Traced every literal to its consumer rather than just grepping for the string — which is how a second, worse bug surfaced: a silent fallback was counting any client without an assigned plan as full mid-tier revenue in the admin's live MRR dashboard. Consolidated pricing into a single source read by both backend and frontend, and made "no valid plan" a value the UI is structurally required to display rather than quietly absorb.
12 hardcoded price points → 1 source of truth
Silent MRR inflation caught: 7.7× overstated in one real scenario
Outcome
Zero hardcoded prices remain, and the business dashboard stopped reporting revenue from clients who weren't generating it. Same-day close, with regression tests written before the initiative was marked done.
What I'd do differently
A one-line question turned into a two-bug fix. Small corrections that touch a number a client sees deserve the same scrutiny as a new feature — more, because they don't feel like one.

Winning an infrastructure investment from a skeptical CEO
Platform / Unit Economics
Challenge
The CEO doubted the return on building owned infrastructure (Kubernetes + Terraform) instead of staying on managed hosting. Effort, cost, and value were all under question — and the honest answer to "is it cheaper?" was no.
Approach
Base hosting cost was nearly identical between the two options, so arguing on price would have lost. Reframed the case around unit economics instead: every client needs at least one staging branch and a fifth of the portfolio needs a second, and on managed hosting each one carried a recurring per-branch fee. Owned infrastructure absorbs them at no marginal cost.
Marginal cost per additional staging branch: ~€12/mo → €0
Portfolio scaling cost: linear → flat
Outcome
Investment approved and in production. It's now the base layer the SaaS platform runs on — the infrastructure decision turned into a product capability.
What I'd do differently
Effort tracking formally belonged to another manager and never got picked up. I owned the architecture and assumed the reporting would follow; the estimate slipped and the CEO had to ask for status rather than receive it. Real ownership includes giving stakeholders visibility before they come looking for it.

Compounddomain depth across verticals
Designing production ERP across three manufacturing verticals
ERP / Manufacturing
Challenge
Five industrial clients whose manufacturing logic had almost nothing in common: panel-based house construction (discrete assembly), packaged food production (process manufacturing), and metal profile fabrication from coil, with shop-floor PLC integration.
Approach
End-to-end solution design rather than configuration — MRP, inventory control, and production orders with materials planning modelled to each vertical's actual business rules. In the metal fabrication case, the work extended past the management layer into direct PLC integration on the plant floor.
5 clients across 3 distinct manufacturing patterns
Discrete · process · PLC-instrumented
Outcome
Domain fluency that's hard to fake in an interview: not knowing what an MRP is, but having modelled batch traceability and shop-floor instrumentation for businesses that fail differently from each other.
What I'd do differently
Solved each client's problem well, but never turned five implementations into a product-level view of what the manufacturing segment actually needs. That's the jump from executing a domain to owning it — and it's exactly the one I'm making now.

The CEO
Budget holder
Doubted the ROI of building our own infrastructure.
Base cost was genuinely a wash, so I stopped arguing price and rebuilt the case on unit economics of scaling. Winning required conceding the point he was right about first.
A systems engineer
Peer, another team
Refused to trust my provisioning flow over a security risk.
Went and read the exact code path rather than defending the design. He was right. Redesigned the trust boundary — losing the only committed technical stakeholder would have cost far more than being wrong.
A sales colleague
Internal user
